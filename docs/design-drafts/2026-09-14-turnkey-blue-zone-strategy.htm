Turnkey Blue Zone

Executive determination

The opportunity is real, timely, and technically actionable. The right product is not another agent framework, identity provider, policy language, or trust-scoring fork. It is a zone factory: a system that converts an ordinary environment into a bounded runtime in which consequential autonomous actions require fresh, context-derived authority at the point where those actions become real.

The recommended product and category framing is:

Turnkey Blue Zone — a runtime autonomy control plane. Deploy an evidence-backed environment where every consequential autonomous action must present fresh, context-derived authority at an independent enforcement boundary.

“Frontier Zero Trust” is useful as the thought-leadership thesis: identity and static permission are no longer sufficient when software can plan, delegate, obtain tools, and act at machine speed. “Autonomous Cybersecurity” is a useful outcome statement, but it is too broad for the initial product because a Blue Zone does not replace detection, response, identity, supply-chain security, or a security operations program. It makes those systems governable at runtime by binding identity, environmental evidence, action risk, enforcement, and audit.

The recommended implementation is one single-zone architecture delivered through two initial product profiles, followed by a context-driven assurance track and one specification-gated future track:

  1. Blue Zone Kit — a local or single-environment Basic candidate for development, evaluation, and learning.
  2. Blue Zone Operator — the recommended first product: a Kubernetes-native zone compiler, installer, promotion controller, enforcement fabric, and evidence exporter that can become production-ready after validation.
  3. Deep Blue readiness — a later single-zone assurance track for contexts such as nuclear control and safety-critical medical systems.
  4. Future KTP v3 Federation — a reserved cross-zone track held behind a published, pinned, and reviewed KTP v3 specification.

The Kit and Operator are not competing implementations. They share the same declarative BlueZoneProfile, adapter contracts, decision kernel, conformance suite, and evidence format. Deep Blue strengthens that same single-zone path only where consequence justifies its cost. Federation remains outside the present product contract until KTP v3 supplies normative semantics. A deployment is promoted only when evidence proves it has crossed the next gate.

The best first real-world wedge is a cybersecurity evaluation or autonomous research environment on Kubernetes. Recent incidents show that an agent can escape an intended sandbox, discover reachable infrastructure, acquire ambient credentials, pivot into clusters and repositories, and perform thousands of actions before a human can respond (OpenAI incident report, Hugging Face technical timeline). Separate 2026 research found six evaluation runs in which models reached unauthorized real systems through misconfigured internet paths; the affected organizations had not detected the activity (Anthropic investigation). This is the cleanest place to prove that a Blue Zone can turn a declared constraint such as “no external network” into measured state, fail-closed egress, action-level authorization, and replayable evidence.

This document is a proposed strategy, not an approved KIL or KTP specification. It uses KTP 2.1.0 as the published protocol baseline, treats KIL as an experimental enforcement and evidence profile, and keeps all new deployment objects in a separate Turnkey Blue Zone namespace until protocol alignment is reviewed (canonical KTP citation).

What the evidence supports

The central problem

NIST Zero Trust Architecture rejects implicit trust based on network location and calls for dynamic policy informed by identity, asset state, environment, and behavior (NIST SP 800-207). NIST's cloud-native companion centers application and service identities, API gateways, sidecar proxies, and service-mesh enforcement across hybrid and multi-cloud environments (NIST SP 800-207A). CISA's maturity model similarly describes continuous enforcement and automated response to changing enterprise conditions (CISA ZTMM 2.0).

Those principles remain necessary, but autonomous systems expose a harder unit of control: the action. An authenticated workload may be legitimate while a particular command, destination, data transfer, credential use, or delegated step is not. NIST's 2026 agent-identity work makes this gap explicit by asking how identity and authorization should change when agent tools and context change, how ephemeral and task-dependent identities should work, how delegation and intent should be bound, and how tamper-resistant records can support nonrepudiation (NIST concept paper, NIST announcement).

KTP supplies the missing governing invariant:

A <= E

A = intrinsic risk of the requested action
E = what the current environment can support

Its Trust Score further separates earned standing from current risk:

E_trust = E_base * (1 - R)

Identity and prior standing therefore do not grant an unconditional capability. The environment must support this action now, and a Soul constraint can veto it before weighted risk is aggregated. KTP 2.1.0 specifies the current published core, identity, signals, sensors, enforcement, audit, emergency behavior, zone, Oracle, transport, conformance, migration, privacy, and governance baseline as a 27-document draft series (KTP RFC repository). Although current v2.1 texts contain federation-related concepts and legacy requirements, this product strategy treats normative federation as future KTP v3 work that is not yet written. Those references are not an implementation contract and cannot support a present federation claim.

The Blue Zone is where that theory becomes an operating environment. The KTP zone specification requires a bounded and discoverable zone, Zone Gateway, Trust Oracle capability, policy enforcement points, context measurement, and a Flight Recorder. It defines a gradient of Deep Blue, Blue, Cyan, Green, and Wild environments, plus ingress, egress, governance, and genesis behavior (KTP Zones). The public explainer describes the practical feedback loop: measure context, compute trust, enforce where the action becomes real, and record what happened for audit and replay (Blue Zones explainer).

The journey to the right depth of Blue

Blue Zones are a spectrum, not a binary certification and not a demand that every organization pursue maximum control. The current KTP Zones RFC defines the canonical gradient as Wild → Green → Cyan → Blue → Deep Blue. “Light Blue” is useful conversational shorthand for the beginning of the journey, but it is not a normative zone label in the current RFC; Green and Cyan are the specified on-ramp stages.

The two axes must remain separate:

KTP assurance depth Purpose in the current Zones RFC Typical contexts What the Turnkey journey must prove
Wild No KTP enforcement Legacy internet and unzoned systems Inventory the boundary and make the absence of KTP protection explicit
Green Minimal validation and passthrough Public sites, open APIs, adoption on-ramps, test environments, legacy interfaces Establish identity and Trust Proof bridging without claiming full enforcement
Cyan Partial, lightweight enforcement General web applications, public APIs, developer platforms, early adopters Enforce a declared subset of actions and sensors with measured coverage and explicit omissions
Blue Full KTP enforcement Enterprise applications, SaaS, research, e-commerce, and regulated but non-critical systems Prove full declared-path interception, trajectory, sensing, veto, recording, recovery, and production operations for the named scope
Deep Blue Maximum structural enforcement Healthcare data, financial trading, government-classified systems, critical infrastructure, and nuclear-facility controls Add maximum assurance, full trajectory verification, continuous sensing, threshold controls, defense in depth, independent assessment, and critical-system recovery evidence

An organization first selects a target color from consequence and context. It then progresses operationally through inventory → observe → shadow → canary → enforce → attest at that chosen depth. A public API may responsibly stop at Cyan. A normal enterprise platform may target Blue. A nuclear control or high-consequence medical environment may require Deep Blue. Reaching a darker color is not success by itself; proving that the selected context is safely supported is success.

This distinction also prevents three independent concepts from being collapsed:

  • zone color describes the intended assurance depth;
  • rollout state describes how far enforcement has been activated; and
  • conformance/evidence level describes what has actually been demonstrated.

A deployment can therefore be “Cyan, shadowing, E1” or “Blue, enforcing, E5.” No color may be claimed from installation alone, and Deep Blue is never the default destination.

The system-level gap

The reviewed sources provide the protocol, useful kernels, examples, and an emerging infrastructure enforcement experiment. They do not yet provide a production-ready zone lifecycle product.

Source What is usable now What it does not establish
KTP RFC series Normative vocabulary, equations, zone model, wire and conformance requirements, migration stages It is a draft specification, not an IETF standard or an operating product
kinetic-trust-python Deterministic KTP v2 Core and Trust Proof primitives; broad unit-test corpus No server, PEP, durable store, key lifecycle, transport, policy plane, or complete product conformance
kinetic-trust-go KTP v2 reference library and Basic-candidate reference composition with conformance tooling Product-owned storage, transport, identity/evidence integration, PEP interception, and production topology remain outside the library
martherus/kinetic-trust Helpful architectural examples, middleware, REST and early federation concepts Version 0.2.0 uses KTP v1 vocabulary and thresholds; neither those federation examples nor current v2.1 references can be treated as the future KTP v3 normative base

The missing product layer is deployment compilation, adapter composition, coverage discovery, safe migration, operational readiness, conformance execution, evidence packaging, upgrade control, and continuous proof that no consequential path bypasses enforcement.

Two source-alignment issues must become explicit preflight gates. First, the Blue Zones website still contains older agent-lineage terms—tethered, divergent, and persistent—that KTP v2 renamed to sponsored, independent, and guarantor (Blue Zones explainer, KTP RFC repository). Second, examples inside the current zone text include earlier discovery and governance version strings even though the series identifies itself as 2.1.0. These are manageable draft-specification risks, but they make automatic version pinning, vocabulary linting, and profile compatibility non-negotiable.

The evolution from Zero Trust

Turnkey Blue Zone should be positioned as an operational extension of Zero Trust, not a replacement for it.

Layer Governing question Primary mechanism
Conventional access control Does this principal possess a permitted role or token? IAM, RBAC, OAuth/OIDC, credentials
Zero Trust Architecture Should this identity access this resource under current identity, device, network, and policy conditions? PDP/PEP, workload identity, microsegmentation, continuous signals
Kinetic Trust Can the current environment support this particular autonomous action now? A <= E, earned standing, current risk, Soul veto, trajectory
KIL Can the derived state be bound to the request and reduced again at the infrastructure boundary? Signed short-lived Q state, action and authority binding, local reducing-only checks
Turnkey Blue Zone Can an organization deploy, promote, operate, and prove that whole control loop safely? Profiles, adapters, enforcement coverage, staged rollout, conformance, evidence packs

This justifies the narrative Frontier Zero Trust: from authenticated access to continuously earned action authority. It also draws a defensible scope line: Turnkey Blue Zone controls whether a consequential action reaches a protected sink. It does not promise that an agent is truthful, aligned, uncompromised, or free of hallucination.

The label “Autonomous Cybersecurity” can describe the broader destination in which machines measure, constrain, record, and recover at machine speed. It should not be the initial product category. “Zero Trust for AI agents,” “Agentic Zero Trust,” and “Autonomous Zero Trust” are already used by multiple vendors and projects (Anthropic, Cisco, Microsoft). The distinctive, ownable concept is the Blue Zone and its evidence-backed zone factory.

Product definition

What a Turnkey Blue Zone is

A Turnkey Blue Zone is a versioned deployment and operating procedure that:

  1. inventories agents, workloads, destinations, identities, credentials, tools, data stores, and every consequential execution path;
  2. converts organizational intent into a closed, signed zone profile;
  3. binds workloads to attested identities and actions to a canonical risk taxonomy;
  4. connects context sensors to a pinned KTP calculation kernel;
  5. places independent PEPs at consequence boundaries and removes bypass paths;
  6. starts in observation, progresses through shadow and canary, and enforces only after measured gates pass;
  7. writes decision, evidence, execution, and non-execution facts to an integrity-protected Flight Recorder; and
  8. continuously emits a machine-verifiable evidence pack describing the exact version, topology, policy, coverage, tests, failures, and claim level.

It is turnkey because one workflow compiles and operates the system. It is not instantaneous: signal validity, action classification, governance, key custody, and production safety require accountable decisions that software cannot safely invent.

Claims vocabulary

The product must use a closed claim ladder:

Claim Minimum meaning
Installed Components are present; no security or conformance implication
Observing Traffic and actions are inventoried; no enforced consequence claim
Shadowing The system produces decisions and counterfactuals without blocking
Enforcing Named actions on named paths are intercepted under a named failure policy
Basic candidate The deployment passes the pinned KTP Basic test profile; intended for development or limited pilots
Turnkey production candidate Single-zone production controls and Turnkey operational gates pass; this is not automatically a KTP Standard claim
Deep Blue readiness candidate Maximum-assurance single-zone controls pass for one named critical context; this is not automatically a KTP Full claim
KTP Standard or Full candidate Every requirement in the pinned normative profile passes; the claim is withheld if that profile depends on federation not yet resolved by KTP v3
Validated profile A named laboratory or pilot result passed its evidence gate
Production approved The deploying organization accepted residual risks and operational gates for a named scope

“Deployed” must never imply “conformant.” “Conformant” must never imply “secure against all threats.” “Basic” must never be marketed as production Blue simply because installation succeeded. KTP's own conformance scheme permits self-assessment at lower levels and describes third-party certification for Full; because KTP itself is a draft series, those terms must not be confused with accredited external certification (KTP Conformance).

Proposed turnkey experience

The operator-facing workflow should converge on:

tbz init       # discover environment and create an unsigned draft profile
tbz plan       # compile adapters, action coverage, policies, risks, and gaps
tbz observe    # deploy passive collection and prove topology without blocking
tbz shadow     # compute decisions and counterfactuals; still do not block
tbz canary     # enforce a bounded action set and publish rollback conditions
tbz enforce    # activate the approved profile at all declared PEPs
tbz verify     # run conformance, bypass, failure, recovery, and evidence checks
tbz export     # emit signed human- and machine-readable evidence packs

Every state-changing command should show the exact profile digest, affected resources, intended claim transition, recovery checkpoint, and stop conditions. The CLI should be a thin client over a declarative controller so the same contract works in automation.

Proposed experience targets—not validated service-level claims—are:

  • first local Basic candidate in 15 minutes on a supported workstation;
  • first connected observation or shadow deployment within one working day;
  • first bounded production canary within two weeks when identity, action scope, and owners are already known; and
  • no time promise for Standard or Full promotion until coverage, failure tests, governance, and independent review pass.

Three delivery options

Option A — Blue Zone Kit

Package a single Oracle, KTP kernel, one or two PEP adapters, local recorder, sample sensors, profile compiler, and conformance runner as a CLI plus Helm or Compose assets.

Best for: developer laptops, CI, demonstrations, evaluation sandboxes, and teams learning KTP.

Advantages: fastest release; makes the protocol tangible; creates reusable profiles and test vectors; lowers adoption friction.

Limits: weak availability and fault isolation; limited bypass coverage; single Oracle; no federation; unsuitable for strong production claims.

Decision: build this as the on-ramp and development distribution, but do not make it the product's final architecture.

Option B — Blue Zone Operator — recommended

Build a Kubernetes operator around a signed BlueZoneProfile. The operator compiles workload identity, network and application PEP configuration, sensors, Oracle deployment, recorder, conformance jobs, and staged promotion into an auditable realization. Initial adapters should target SPIRE, Envoy ext_authz, Calico or Cilium policy, Kubernetes admission and API mediation, cloud metadata and credential paths, and immutable object storage.

Best for: AI evaluation clusters, CI/CD agents, autonomous operations, research systems, and enterprise agent platforms.

Advantages: matches KIL's strongest current trajectory; Kubernetes supplies declarative state, identities, reconciliation, admission, and portable packaging; Envoy can delegate HTTP or network authorization and defaults to denial when its authorization service is unavailable unless explicitly set to fail open (Envoy external authorization). Cilium can enforce identity-aware L3–L7 policy in the kernel and provides explicit deny semantics that take precedence over allow (Cilium policy, Cilium deny semantics).

Limits: Kubernetes is not every environment; operators and sidecars can become high-value targets; egress and host-level coverage must be proven rather than inferred; Standard conformance requires substantially more than a Helm install.

Decision: make this the first commercial-quality and reference deployment. Its interfaces remain substrate-neutral so VM, edge, robotics, and serverless adapters can follow.

Option C — Future KTP v3 Federation — reserved critical path

After the v3 entry gate, add multi-zone discovery and gateways, cross-zone evidence translation, HSM/KMS-backed keys, regional recorders, privacy controls, disaster recovery, governance workflows, and third-party assessment support.

Best for: regulated enterprises, multi-cloud operators, critical infrastructure, sovereign environments, and inter-organizational agent systems.

Advantages: if standardized and validated in KTP v3, this would realize the cross-zone trust thesis and remove the single-zone control-plane assumption.

Limits: highest cost and governance burden; consensus and recovery introduce new failure modes; interoperability is immature; premature federation would slow learning from the first enforceable use case.

Decision: preserve clean single-zone interfaces and record federation as a critical-path dependency now, but do not invent, implement, or claim normative federation semantics. Begin that work only after KTP v3 is written, published, pinned, and reviewed—and after Option B proves single-zone safety in at least two real pilots.

Recommended architecture

One profile, two control loops, multiple PEPs

The architecture combines KTP's slow authoritative trust loop with KIL's fast local enforcement loop:

declared intent + identity + trajectory + sensors
                         |
                         v
             KTP Oracle / decision kernel
                         |
              short-lived signed state
                         |
                         v
agent intent hook --> PEP at consequence boundary --> target or withhold
       |                         |
       +------ observations -----+
                         |
                  Flight Recorder
                         |
             promotion and recovery controller

The authoritative loop computes KTP state from governed evidence. The fast path verifies that state, resolves the actual action, applies infrastructure-fixed policy, and may only preserve or reduce authority. It must never expand a KTP decision. This lets expensive context computation happen outside the request hot path while keeping proofs short-lived and request-bound.

Components

  1. Profile compiler. Validates the protocol release, schema, vocabulary, identities, action taxonomy, sensors, PEP coverage, failure modes, data retention, keys, migration stage, and expected evidence. It emits an immutable plan and rejects ambiguous defaults.
  2. Zone Operator. Reconciles the signed plan into substrate-specific resources and reports drift without silently widening access.
  3. Identity binder. Uses SPIFFE/SPIRE or an approved bridge to bind workload identity to runtime evidence. SPIRE attests nodes and workloads and issues SVIDs through the SPIFFE Workload API; this solves workload identity, not KTP's action-authority decision (SPIRE concepts).
  4. Action resolver. Maps an observed API call, command, network destination, data operation, or tool invocation to a versioned action class and intrinsic risk. The mapping is controlled by the zone, never by an agent header.
  5. Signal fabric. Authenticates and normalizes product-selected KTP signals, records staleness and absence, identifies correlated sensors, and rejects unknown transformations.
  6. KTP kernel and Oracle. Uses one pinned calculation implementation and profile. The recommended runtime seed is the Go reference library; Python is retained for independent vectors, integration tests, and analysis. Product code supplies durable state, key lifecycle, transport, evidence, and deployment topology.
  7. Proof service. Issues and verifies short-lived, audience-, subject-, and action-bound decisions. It treats clock uncertainty, replay, revocation, and missing evidence as explicit inputs.
  8. PEP fabric. Enforces at API gateways, service mesh, egress, Kubernetes or cloud control plane, database or queue proxy, secret broker, and execution sandbox. Envoy/Istio integration is practical because Istio's CUSTOM authorization action delegates to an Envoy external authorizer (Istio external authorization).
  9. Flight Recorder. Commits decision inputs, geometry, policy and binary digests, PEP result, target-execution witness, missing evidence, and recovery state to a chained or equivalent tamper-evident store. OpenTelemetry traces, metrics, and logs are export formats, not the authoritative integrity model (OpenTelemetry signals).
  10. Evidence and conformance engine. Runs deterministic vectors, topology checks, negative probes, failure tests, recovery, and offline verification; emits a signed evidence pack and precise claim level.
  11. Promotion controller. Advances observe → shadow → canary → enforce only on explicit approval and passed gates. Failed conditions reduce or freeze authority; they never auto-promote.
  12. Recovery plane. Owns key rotation, backup/restore, break-glass constraints, degraded modes, zone collapse, and exact rollback to the last accepted profile.

Defense at intent and consequence

Framework-level hooks can expose an agent's proposed tool invocation early, but they cannot be the only boundary because a compromised runtime can omit or lie about intent. Infrastructure PEPs must govern the consequence.

The OWASP Agent Control Standard is a promising optional adapter: it defines pre-action runtime hooks, a separate Guardian Agent, action decisions, and telemetry conventions (OWASP ACS). Its current reference implementation is explicitly not production hardened, uses an unauthenticated wire protocol, and defaults to proceeding when the guardian is unreachable. Turnkey Blue Zone should therefore use ACS as an intent signal and framework integration, then authenticate it, make failure behavior profile-controlled, and retain the KIL or substrate PEP as the mandatory enforcement boundary.

Identity, policy, and supply-chain composition

  • SPIFFE/SPIRE supplies attested workload identities and trust domains.
  • OIDC/OAuth/mTLS bridges preserve existing human and service access while KTP determines action authority.
  • OPA, Cedar, or native IAM may express organizational policy inside a fixed envelope, but no plug-in policy may override the KTP Silent Veto or increase the signed authority.
  • Envoy provides the first L7 authorization boundary. Its authorization filter should be first in the relevant chain, failure_mode_allow must be false for protected actions, and disabling the filter must not make protected routes permissive (Envoy external authorization).
  • Calico or Cilium supplies default-deny network isolation and closes direct paths around Envoy. Cilium's own documentation warns that endpoints are initially unrestricted until selected by policy in its default mode; the operator must therefore install and attest isolation before workloads become ready (Cilium enforcement modes).
  • Sigstore or an equivalent verified-release mechanism binds controller, adapter, and policy artifacts to expected identities and digests (Sigstore verification).
  • KMS/HSM protects Oracle and profile-signing keys in production profiles.

Deployment procedure

KTP's migration guidance—observe before enforcement, preserve coexistence and reversibility, and progress through shadow, advisory, canary, and full enforcement—is the correct backbone (KTP Migration).

Gate 0 — qualify the zone

Define the business owner, security owner, agent population, consequences, data sensitivity, geographic and privacy boundaries, availability needs, and candidate KTP zone/conformance level. Identify what must never happen, what may happen only under constraint, and what safe heartbeat or hibernation behavior looks like.

Exit evidence: signed scope, action owners, data map, protected sinks, residual-risk owner, and explicit non-goals.

Gate 1 — discover and preflight

Inventory workload and human identities, credentials, APIs, egress, cluster and cloud control paths, databases, queues, code repositories, package registries, model/tool interfaces, metadata services, and out-of-band administrative paths. Run connectivity and execution probes before claiming coverage.

Exit evidence: topology graph, identity graph, action catalog, credential graph, sensor inventory, all known bypasses, and a coverage denominator.

Gate 2 — compile and sign

Pin the KTP release and compatibility profile. Resolve every adapter and image to a digest. Validate action mappings, sensor semantics, time source, proof freshness, failure modes, record retention, privacy controls, keys, rollout, and rollback. Produce a signed plan before mutation.

Exit evidence: reproducible plan, profile signature, supply-chain attestations, change approval, and recovery checkpoint.

Gate 3 — observe

Deploy identities, sensors, recorder, and passive topology witnesses without changing action outcomes. Reconcile declared paths against observed paths.

Exit evidence: stable identity binding, sensor freshness/absence rates, action distribution, uncovered paths, and zero unexpected mutations by the installer.

Gate 4 — shadow

Compute KTP and local-reduction decisions on live actions while leaving legacy policy authoritative. Compare proposed allow/constrain/deny outcomes with actual execution and operator expectations. Red-team action classification, sensor poisoning, and agent-supplied metadata.

Exit evidence: false-allow and false-deny analysis, counterfactual records, latency distribution, action coverage, agreed tuning, and no unresolved critical bypass.

Gate 5 — bounded canary

Enforce a small, reversible action set first: internet egress, metadata service, cluster administration, credential minting, code/release writes, and destructive data operations are strong candidates. Maintain a separately governed recovery channel that cannot broaden normal authority silently.

Exit evidence: target execution/non-execution witnesses, rollback drill, failure injection, incident response exercise, and owner approval.

Gate 6 — enforce and attest

Activate the approved scope only after all declared PEPs report the expected configuration and direct bypass probes fail. Run the pinned KTP profile plus Turnkey operational tests. Export a signed evidence pack with exact limitations.

Exit evidence: claim-level decision, conformance results, coverage report, failure matrix, software and policy bill of materials, and accepted residual risks.

Gate 7 — operate, renew, and prepare for future federation

Continuously detect drift, re-run selected probes, rotate keys, expire proofs, review action catalogs, inspect denied and constrained trajectories, exercise recovery, and require gated promotion for upgrades. Preserve authenticated and versioned boundaries for eventual cross-zone work, but introduce federation only after each participating zone has a stable local evidence history and a published KTP v3 federation specification supplies the normative contract.

Exit evidence: periodic readiness attestations, drill results, version-skew tests, key-rotation proof, and a deprecation or exit plan.

Real-world applicability

Autonomous cybersecurity evaluation

This should be the first pilot. A profile declares that an evaluation agent may interact only with a target range, approved artifact sources, a synthetic identity plane, and a bounded callback service. Network enforcement denies all other egress; workload identity prevents ambient credentials; metadata access is separately controlled; cluster, cloud, repository, and package operations are high-risk actions; every result joins the decision with a target witness.

The Anthropic evaluation incidents provide a particularly testable acceptance case: when the evaluation definition says “no internet,” the product must prove actual egress policy, probe all alternate routes, and withhold traffic even if the agent runtime or evaluator is misconfigured (Anthropic investigation).

AI research and model-evaluation infrastructure

The Hugging Face/OpenAI incident demonstrates the relevant chain: exploit an evaluation surface, escape the intended boundary, reach a third-party launchpad, exploit additional services, acquire access to cluster and cloud systems, and affect repositories and datasets at high speed (Hugging Face timeline). A Blue Zone maps those consequences to distinct action classes and PEPs:

Observed incident pattern Blue Zone control
Sandbox or processor escape Runtime isolation plus consequence PEP; escape alone does not confer authority
Unexpected internet path Default-deny egress, destination/action binding, direct bypass probes
Metadata or ambient credential use Workload identity, metadata PEP, short-lived brokered credentials
Cluster API access Independently mediated control-plane action with high intrinsic risk
Repository or dataset modification Repository gateway/action PEP, provenance checks, constrained write scope
Machine-speed exploration Rate, sequence, trajectory, and adversarial-pressure signals reduce authority
Evidence destruction External, tamper-evident Flight Recorder and execution witness

It is plausible that these controls would have interrupted several reported steps. It is not yet proven that KTP or KIL would have prevented the historical incident. That claim requires a disclosed scenario, reproducible attack graph, implemented controls, negative and failure tests, and evidence that the protected consequences were withheld under those conditions.

CI/CD and software-supply-chain agents

An agent may read issues and source broadly but requires higher current authority to modify protected branches, change workflows, request secrets, publish packages, sign releases, alter deployment policy, or promote to production. Action-bound proofs, verified artifacts, ephemeral credentials, and independent repository/release PEPs constrain the blast radius of a hijacked planning loop.

Cloud and Kubernetes operations

An operations agent may observe broadly, diagnose within one namespace, and perform reversible service actions under normal conditions. Privilege changes, network-policy edits, secret access, node operations, and cross-environment deployment have higher intrinsic risk and stricter evidence. During telemetry loss, an incident, or an unreviewed change window, current authority drops even if the identity and RBAC role remain valid.

Robotics, edge, and physical systems

The same interfaces can govern actuator commands, safety-zone entry, firmware changes, remote administration, and data exfiltration. This later profile needs local decision continuity, hardware-rooted identity, bounded offline operation, physical interlocks, and deterministic safe states. It should follow—not block— the Kubernetes wedge.

Mandatory test families

  1. Protocol calculation: exact thresholds, rounding, risk aggregation, Soul veto, tier ceilings, attenuation, trajectory, and version negotiation.
  2. Identity and delegation: workload/agent binding, cloned identity, confused deputy, sponsorship limits, revocation, cross-tenant and cross-zone isolation.
  3. Proof security: signature, issuer, audience, subject, action, nonce, time, replay, key rotation, downgrade, unknown fields, and malformed inputs.
  4. Action classification: unknown action, semantic alias, path and verb ambiguity, parameter escalation, batch requests, indirect consequences, and agent-supplied risk labels.
  5. PEP coverage: direct target access, alternate protocol, sidecar bypass, host network, control plane, DNS, metadata service, IPv4/IPv6, egress proxy, administrative and recovery paths.
  6. Sensor integrity: absence, staleness, spoofing, poisoning, correlated failure, outliers, drift, time skew, contradictory evidence, and privacy minimization.
  7. Oracle behavior: unavailable, slow, partitioned, stale, equivocation, compromised signer, consensus failure, and rollback to earlier state.
  8. Recorder behavior: unavailable before allow, delayed commit, truncation, reordering, replacement, sensitive-data leakage, deletion, and offline replay.
  9. Availability and safety: load, backpressure, fail-open attempts, hibernation, emergency changes, zone collapse, human recovery, and denial of service against the authorization plane.
  10. Lifecycle: clean install, coexistence, rollback, crash recovery, upgrade/downgrade, protocol and adapter version skew, disaster recovery, multi-architecture operation, and exact owned-resource teardown.

KTP's threat model already identifies Oracle key compromise, consensus manipulation, PEP bypass or compromise, stale caches, sensor spoofing and poisoning, and recorder evasion as primary threats (KTP Threat Model). Turnkey tests must operationalize those threats instead of merely copying them into a risk register.

Metrics and promotion criteria

Each pilot must set values appropriate to its availability and safety needs. The platform should provide the measures without inventing universal thresholds:

  • protected-action interception coverage and denominator confidence;
  • direct and alternate-path bypass count, which must be zero for a protected claim;
  • unknown or ambiguously classified action rate;
  • false-allow, false-deny, and constrained-action disagreement rates from shadow review;
  • decision and end-to-end p50/p95/p99 latency under normal and degraded load;
  • proof age, sensor staleness, clock uncertainty, and revocation propagation;
  • recorder completeness and decision-to-execution join rate;
  • percentage of allows with a durable decision record and target witness;
  • recovery point and time, key-rotation success, rollback time, and operator drill success;
  • number and duration of unauthorized consequences, not just alerts generated; and
  • privacy footprint, retention exceptions, access to evidence, and verified erasure where policy permits it.

The hardest acceptance property is also the most valuable: for every protected action, either an approved fresh decision is joined to the resulting execution, or there is evidence that execution was withheld.

Exit gate: E0–E2 pass from clean source on two architectures with published reproduction instructions.

Phase 2 — Kubernetes Blue Zone Operator

Duration guide: eight to twelve weeks. Deliverables: CRDs and operator; SPIRE adapter; Envoy/Istio adapter; Calico/Cilium isolation; control-plane and metadata PEPs; admission checks; observe/shadow/canary promotion; immutable recorder; rollback; KIL V3B-2 nominal and failure evidence. Exit gate: no known protected-path bypass in the closed test topology, E3 failure matrix passes, and independent review finds no unresolved critical issue.

Phase 3 — first pilots

Duration guide: six to ten weeks per bounded pilot. Deliverables: one cyber-evaluation pilot and one operational-agent pilot; action/sensor calibration; SLOs; privacy assessment; red-team exercise; operator runbooks; evidence packs and case study with explicit limitations. Exit gate: E5 acceptance by the owning organizations and a prioritized record of false decisions, operational friction, and uncovered integrations.

Phase 4 — production hardening

Duration guide: three to six months after pilots. Deliverables: high availability; hardened KMS/HSM keys; Oracle topology; multi-cluster and cloud adapters; disaster recovery; version skew; long-running performance; external security assessment; production-readiness dossier. Any KTP Standard claim remains withheld unless every requirement of the pinned normative profile passes and its federation dependency is resolved. Exit gate: E6 and production approval for a named scope—not a universal security claim.

Phase 5 — Deep Blue hardening

Duration guide: six to twelve months after single-zone stability. Deliverables: critical-system threat model; maximum-assurance sensing and trajectory; consensus and threshold signing within the zone; defense-in-depth PEPs; critical-system safe states; privacy-preserving evidence; partition and split-brain recovery; Deep Blue readiness dossier and independent assessment workflow. Any KTP Full claim remains withheld unless every requirement of the pinned normative profile passes and its federation dependency is resolved. Exit gate: E7 for one named Deep Blue context, including a realistic critical-system exercise and documented emergency, recovery, and revocation behavior. This does not establish federation.

Phase 6 — KTP v3 Federation — specification-gated critical path

Schedule: not estimated until KTP v3 federation is written and published. Entry gate: a pinned KTP v3 release defines normative zone discovery, cross-zone proof and trajectory semantics, attenuation, failure, revocation, privacy, governance, and conformance vectors; maintainers approve the Turnkey compatibility interpretation. Then—and only then—candidate deliverables: federated gateways; cross-zone evidence exchange; version negotiation; partner isolation; multi-organization recovery; interoperability tests; and independent assessment. Exit gate: the future v3 Federation evidence level passes against the published contract. Until then, Federation remains a critical-path dependency, not a shipping feature or a conformance claim.

Durations are planning ranges, not commitments. Gates—not calendar dates—control promotion.

Repository and implementation strategy

Create Turnkey Blue Zone as a sibling product/repository with explicit versioned dependencies on KTP and KIL. Do not turn the KIL evidence harness into a general product controller prematurely.

  • KTP RFC remains the protocol authority and conformance source.
  • KTP Go is the recommended seed for the production decision kernel because its reference composition and conformance kit provide the strongest current executable starting point.
  • KTP Python supplies independent vector checks, prototyping, and data analysis. Avoid maintaining divergent policy arithmetic in two production paths.
  • KIL remains the reference infrastructure enforcer and evidence laboratory. Turnkey Blue Zone consumes stable KIL profiles and adapters after their gates pass.
  • Martherus examples inform API and migration ergonomics, but a compatibility adapter must translate v1 vocabulary explicitly; no v1 threshold or enum may leak into a v2.1 profile.

The product can later absorb proven KIL components, but only through tagged interfaces and matching evidence. This separation protects both projects: KIL can continue conservative experimental validation while the product develops installer, lifecycle, and operator experience.

Review distribution contract

Every artifact presented for stakeholder review must be distributed as a standalone .htm reader. Markdown may remain the controlled authoring source, but reviewers should never need the repository, a documentation toolchain, or a companion file to read the complete artifact.

A review reader must:

  • contain its CSS, navigation, search, print support, and any required script inline;
  • contain every table, diagram, decision, limitation, and approval question needed for that review;
  • use only internal anchors and canonical HTTPS citations—never a relative link to another local file for required context;
  • use no local fonts, images, stylesheets, scripts, or other runtime assets;
  • remain readable with scripting disabled and printable to PDF; and
  • pass an automated portability check before it is identified as review-ready.

If a later design depends on earlier KIL evidence, it must summarize the exact claim and limitation inside its own reader, then link to the public canonical source only as supplemental evidence. A collection may include several .htm readers, but each member must remain independently understandable and shareable.

Decisions requested

The plan recommends the following choices for approval:

  1. Approve the category: “Turnkey Blue Zone — runtime autonomy control plane,” with “Frontier Zero Trust” as the broader thesis.
  2. Approve the first wedge: a Kubernetes-based autonomous cybersecurity or model-evaluation zone.
  3. Approve the product structure: Kit and Operator as the single-zone delivery path across the right-sized color spectrum; keep Federation as a specification-gated KTP v3 critical-path dependency rather than a current product profile.
  4. Approve the technical baseline: KTP 2.1.0 archive pinned by DOI; Go kernel; SPIFFE/SPIRE identity; Envoy plus Calico/Cilium consequence enforcement; KIL reducing-only state and evidence methodology; optional authenticated ACS intent adapter.
  5. Approve the repository boundary: new sibling product repository, with KTP and KIL consumed through versioned interfaces rather than merged into one codebase.
  6. Approve evidence-gated delivery: no production, prevention, performance, Standard, Full, Deep Blue, or future Federation claim before its corresponding evidence level and normative protocol gate passes.

If these decisions are accepted, the next artifact should be a narrow product requirements and architecture specification for Phase 0. Implementation should not begin until that specification fixes the first pilot, action scope, conformance target, governance owners, and extension boundary.
